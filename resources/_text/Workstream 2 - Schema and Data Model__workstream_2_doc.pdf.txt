2026-08-10 TEA-seq analysis exercise – Thoughts & Comments
By Aishwarya Chander

Gaps for multi-omic, multimodal assays:

   1.​ Need more modalities: The modality list is mostly neural dynamics assays and is
       missing most assays that Immunology uses (FLOW, OLINK, CITE-seq, TEA-seq etc).
       acquisition_type is free text with no controlled vocabulary. "TEA-seq", "CITE-seq",
       "multiome", and "Flex" all need defined terms to be searchable.
   2.​ No way to capture multiplexed / hashed pooled data: Many 10x methods pool
       multiple donors into one lane and demultiplexes afterward. It's unclear where this should
       be logged — by experiment, donor, or acquisition. There is no field for pool ID, pool size,
       multiplexing method, sample tag assignments, or demultiplexing outputs.
   3.​ More human subject fields: HumanSubject only has species, sex, year of birth, and
       source. Missing: ethnicity, age at enrollment, diagnosis, cohort, consent, and
       participation, clinical survey data (BMI, smoking, alcohol)
   4.​ Tree structure for multimodal and paired datasets: No way to link related assets (e.g.
       RNA + ATAC + ADT from one run, or paired spatial and single-cell from the same
       tissue). Assets are independent records with no parent–child or sibling relationships.
   5.​ Ontology pinning and versioning: Nice to have
   6.​ HIPAA compliance checks: Rules like age masking (90+ must be reported as "90+",
       not an exact age) are documented but not enforced by validators.

AIND Nice things:

   1.​ Acquisition provenance: The subject → specimen → acquisition chain is well modeled.
   2.​ Data processing lineage: DataLevel and source_data give a clear raw → derived
       chain. processing.json captures software and parameters per step.
   3.​ Collapsible, modular multi-document structure: Each document (subject,
       procedures, instrument, acquisition, QC) has its own schema version and can be
       validated independently.
   4.​ Institutional and organizational metadata: ROR organizations, ORCID investigators,
       and funding records are supported.
   5.​ QC documents: omg LOVE.

By Alina Ott

Here's the consolidated summary of gaps between the mIF example data and the
aind-data-schema:

Subject schema
The schema has no type for histological specimens. All three subject detail types (Mouse,
Human, NonHumanPrimate) describe live subjects; TissueSection needs to be added with
fields for tissue/slide IDs, fixation method, embedding medium, section thickness, and antigen
retrieval parameters. For the human subject type specifically: species is redundant with the
subject type and can be dropped; source is designed around animal acquisition and doesn't
translate to a human collaborator/collection site; year_of_birth needs HIPAA-compliant
handling for subjects over 89 (aggregation, and a mechanism to update this over time). Several
fields present in the data have no home in the schema at all: diagnosis (multi-value, updatable),
ethnicity, race, consent, and cohort — all of which are more naturally attached to the subject than
to the acquisition.

Data description schema
subject_id as the primary key breaks down when an acquisition covers multiple specimens, or
when the meaningful identifier is a sample rather than a subject (e.g., a tissue section or a tube).
The auto-generated name field (subject_id + timestamp) risks collision if two datasets are created
on the same day without a precise time. project_name needs clarification on whether it should
be a human-readable name, an experiment ID like EXP-01367, or both. modalities doesn't
include mIF or any cyclic/multiplexed imaging modality — this is a registry gap, not just a
naming question. tags, source_data, and restrictions all lack guidance: what controlled
vocabulary applies to tags, how source_data gets populated (manual vs. automated), and whether
restrictions should hold a legal document ID or overlap with consent fields already in the
subject schema.

Acquisition schema
The biggest structural gap: DataStream models simultaneous data collection, but Cell DIVE (and
any cyclic assay) is inherently sequential — 10 staining rounds, each with its own operator, date,
antibody plan, reagent lots, deactivation step, and QC flags. There's no model for this cyclic
structure. The antibody panel itself — channel assignments, antibody name, clone, fluorophore,
concentration, lot number, and barcode verification — has no place anywhere in the current
schema. This gap applies beyond mIF: Xenium gene panels and Flex scRNA have the same
problem. subject_details within acquisition is mouse-centric (weight, platform, reward) and
mislabeled — for human tissue experiments the relevant "subject at acquisition" information is
clinical or survey data (e.g., smoking history at time of collection), which needs to be searchable.
ethics_review_id is noted as mouse-schema-specific and not applicable for human subjects.
calibrations and maintenance are questioned as better fits under QC than acquisition.
stimulus_epochs and manipulations are entirely inapplicable to fixed tissue experiments.


Proposed additions (from the comments)
Four new models were flagged as needed: TissueSection (subject type), StainingRound (cyclic
acquisition unit with antibody plan, deactivation, and reagent log), AntibodyChannel
(per-channel reagent metadata with barcode verification), and DeactivationStep (reagent lots
and protocol parameters between rounds). mIF also needs to be added to the modality registry,
with a question about granularity — whether "Cell DIVE" should be its own modality or a
platform-level detail within mIF.

Patch-seq data model mapping observations
Representing patch-clamping as a SpecimenProcedure?
      ●​ Not explicitly modeled as such in the current example, since it initially seemed
         redundant with the Acquisition of electrophysiology data, but it might make
         sense to model it as a procedure that produces the individual cell as an
         output specimen
      ●​ It seems like it could be recorded as a manipulation since it is so closely tied
         to an Acquisition, but then I’m not sure how it gets recorded in other metadata
         as part of the provenance chain (like for images taken of the cell afterwards)


Singular location for data (Metadata.location)
      ●​ Morphological reconstruction generates a few related files (SWC file, marker
         file) but these are currently stored in a directory that contains other (not
         directly related) files.
      ●​ There are also aspects of morphological reconstruction (image annotations)
         that are currently saved directly into a database – I didn’t include those in the
         example, but I’m not sure how to track data recorded that way. We’ve also
         had conversations with scientists who want to track e.g. clinical
         characteristics in a database – how does that get referred to by the registry?
      ●​ The raw transcriptomic data for a Patch-seq cell is a pair of fastq files, but
         those are in a directory with fastq files from many other cells
      ●​ The 63X image stack has individual layers spread across multiple directories
         (which are not in one unifying parent directory)


Coordinate systems
      ●​ An Instrument requires a CoordinateSystem, but it may not be appropriate in
         all cases (I’m not sure what the coordinate system for a sequencing machine
         would be)
     ●​ It wasn’t clear to me how to specify a coordinate system for recording in a
        slice. We keep track of the cell’s depth in the slice, for example.
     ●​ I’m not sure the current coordinate system + PlanarSectioning fields can
        exactly capture how slicing is done for some experiments in practice (like if
        they move in semi-arbitrary bigger distances that aren’t precisely tracked until
        they see particular landmarks). Also not sure if the orientation is exactly
        known for pieces of neurosurgical tissue that get sliced.
Procedure/processing types and names
      ●​ Ended up using “OTHER” in several places for procedure types – these could
         be added to the controlled vocabulary but we’d have to figure out the right set
         of terms for them.
      ●​ Similarly, I wasn’t sure about what convention to use for naming things when
         it wasn’t a controlled vocabulary (procedures, acquisitions, instrument) and
         whether it was more important to be consistent with names within the
         metadata for a given data asset vs being consistent across data assets.


Other information I wasn’t sure where to put
      ●​ We want to know if a recorded cell was positive for a fluorescent reporter –
         maybe could be part of a PatchClamping specimen procedure?

📎 AI Draft
Track C: Q&A

1. At what data granularity do we support registration, and how prescriptive
should we be?

Contributors: Dan, Alina, Nathan, Saskia

Summary: Register at the level of an asset/collection, keep file-level constraints optional and
modality-defined, and stay flexible enough for internal users to define their own groupings.

The current schema puts almost no constraints on files — metadata describes the data without
direct links. Per-modality file standards are being developed in neural dynamics, with schema
infrastructure to support them: a files.json core file storing constraints about what files are
expected, then validating that they exist.

For search, the useful unit is some kind of collection (e.g., scRNA generated from human blood
on healthy subjects) rather than an individual file, though defining a collection may depend on
having information about the individual assets it contains. Users will want both curated data
(which may depend on modality) and raw data, similar to GEO/dbGaP.

Because the primary audience is internal, users will not only want curated data — they will want
to find whatever was recently acquired and define their own collections for intermediate
analyses. The system therefore needs to be granular and flexible. The working position: the
asset is the unit of registration.

Open: minimum searchable fields have not been agreed.

2. What is a data asset?

Contributors: Dan, Alina, Nathan, Aishwarya, Tim

Summary: An asset is the output of a data collection event; "session" is too coupled to one kind
of collection, and the relationship between experiment and asset is not 1:1.

In neural dynamics, one session creates one raw data asset, which is processed into an initial
derived asset; users can then group derived data across sessions or subjects. Multi-subject
support is almost certainly needed to handle data from the other accelerators.

"Session" breaks down across modalities. In Patch-seq, the electrophysiology file is generated
during the recording session, but imaging happens later after additional procedures, and
transcriptomics comes from separate procedures — so a single conceptual experiment spans
multiple collection times. Similarly, it is unclear whether multiomics data is one session or one
session per component.
The broader framing: the asset is the output of a data collection event, and the metadata
describes the conditions of that collection. AIBS is generally turning tissue into numbers; AIND
is turning activity into numbers; immunology appears to do both as part of a single experiment.
There are many examples with no 1:1 relationship between experiment and asset, further
complicated by downstream analysis that turns assets into new assets (also not 1:1).

Internally, users would want to search across many fields (disease state, cohort, project, subject
ID, tissue type, modality); some of these may not be relevant cross-institute (e.g., subject ID).

To do: define a minimally viable "analysis unit" that acts as the foundation for the ontology
across different experimental conditions.

Open: what the expected output of a search is — e.g., a tally of the amount of data with
directions on where to find it.

3. How do we handle large external ontologies that don't fit well into Python
packages?

Contributors: Dan, Alina, Aishwarya, Saskia

Summary: Reference actively supported public ontologies via external APIs rather than
packaging them; the open problem is ingestion into the metadata.

biodata-schema already holds large external registries (e.g., EMAPA) and accesses them via
the OLS4 API (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/). The general pattern works, but it can be made more
robust, and how a Python user "sees" these registries needs more thought.

CELLxGENE's approach (https://github.com/chanzuckerberg/cellxgene-ontology-guide) is worth
referencing. The prior question is why the whole ontology needs to fit into a Python package at
all — a lazily loaded data accessor object holding a snapshot of the complete ontology is a
candidate alternative.

The requirement is to use public ontologies that are actively supported. The remaining
challenge is how to ingest the ontology into the metadata accurately without taxing the system.

Note: FMA is under consideration in immunology, which has not previously used ontologies.

9. What is the versioning / registration strategy? (continuous registration
vs noise)

Contributors: Ray, Doug

Summary: Unresolved — the tension is between a high bar for registration and the
searchability cost of leaving assets unregistered.

One position: the bar for adding downstream analysis artifacts should be high. Intermediate
analysis artifacts should live in Code Ocean or another scratch workspace, and data should only
be registered when it needs to be shared for further analysis, annotation, or publication. This will
likely vary by integration/user.

The counter-argument: artifacts that are not registered are not easily searchable. AIND already
sees the current docb going out of sync with Code Ocean, which makes searching difficult, and
deliberately not registering assets would make that worse.

10. How do we store updates?

Contributors: Doug, Mekhla

Summary: Schema versioning is settled (semver); record-level update history is contested
between "purely additive, no history" and "immutable records with a revision chain."

Schema updates: standard semantic versioning is already established, with major/minor/patch
updates as necessary.
Record updates — position A: we may not need to store updates. There will undoubtedly be
fixes to data (e.g., mouse weight recorded incorrectly), and there is little value in keeping the old
data — with the possible exception of an update log for rare audit cases. Other updates should
be purely additive (e.g., adding a procedure to a subject's procedures record).

Record updates — position B: update history matters, especially for publications, since a
paper would cite a dataset and its metadata as it was at the time of publication. A possible
design: immutable records with a revision chain — each update creates a new record version,
the old one is flagged and given a forward pointer, and the current version is the head of the
chain.

11. Relational DB or document store?

Contributors: Mekhla, Ray, Tim, Doug

Summary: Majority preference is relational, with a strong minority view that the persistence
choice matters less than the API contract and non-duplication of data.

Two explicit votes for relational: a DB storing the single subject.json etc. as a source of truth in
the registry, separate from the final data asset.

The API-first view: what matters is how the API functions at the boundary between ingest and
the persistence layer. Functional requirements include being able to CRUD the individual
entities of the data model, and preferably CRUD a complete instance of the model containing all
its component entities, with assurance of referential integrity across entities within a submission
and between submissions where applicable. If that is supported, the underlying infrastructure is
not a concern. The infra choice should follow expected usage patterns.

The neutral view: no strong opinion on relational vs document store — the most important thing
is that information is not duplicated. Concretely, there should be one record for a given subject,
updated in a single location.

Open: given that the metadata schema is nested/hierarchical, is it actually feasible to store in a
relational DB?




12. Which storage systems must the Registry support?

Contributors: Ray, Doug

Summary: Pointing users to the storage location is sufficient, so an arbitrary number of storage
systems can be supported.
The Registry should point users to data but need not broker access to it — giving people a file
path should be sufficient. On that basis, arbitrary numbers of storage systems can be supported.
A single cloud storage provider (AWS) would certainly make things simpler, but that may be
Workstream 4's decision to make.

13. What is the expectation for ETL into the Registry, given different
persistence layers across accelerators?

Contributors: Mekhla, Ray, Doug, Tim

Summary: Assume multiple accelerator-specific sources in the near term, and draw the
boundary at a per-submitter schema contract rather than reaching into each source system.

This is a critical question with broader implications for how each accelerator collaborates with
the new Central Scientific Computing department. The near-term assumption is that there will be
multiple accelerator-specific data sources that need to be ETL'd to / synced with the Registry.

The proposed boundary: the shared registry should not extend into everybody's LIMS, Airtables,
and Smartsheets. Instead, draw the line at a contract developed with each data submitter
agreeing on a schema for data we will accept. It is the submitters' responsibility to push their
data to the registry in that agreed-upon shape; our responsibility is to provide endpoints shaped
per those contracts and then do the "TL" part of ETL into the single shared registry. Negotiating
those contracts is where the work of harmonizing all the data lives — we will need a
transformation from each submitter's data model into the shape of our shared data model.

Open: how to make the ETL process easier given multiple data sources.




14. How are updates and deletions handled?

Contributors: Tim

Summary: Corrections need a managed path, not silent overwrite — likely either in-model
change tracking or an external audit/provenance log.

If the registry is the source of truth and keeper of provenance, we need a policy for changing
data. Assertions may be made about data based on its properties and propagated downstream
into publications, so we probably cannot just change data after the fact.

Two broad paths:

   1.​ Build change tracking into the model (e.g., a "superseded" status with a pointer to the
       new record). Simpler, but requires readers of the data to be aware of that status.
   2.​ Create an external audit / provenance log.
(Related to the revision-chain proposal in Q10.)

15. Does the data we're ingesting ever include controlled-access data?

Contributors: Tim

Summary: Probably not, since the registry holds metadata rather than raw data — but
metadata can sometimes make raw data identifiable, so a policy is needed.

If controlled-access-adjacent metadata must be part of what the registry manages, our ingest
logging, audit trails, and error messages become more complicated and probably less useful.

Open: what our policy will be for holding this kind of metadata in the registry.
